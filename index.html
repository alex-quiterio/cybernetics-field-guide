A guided tour with working models

Cybernetics Field Guide

Cybernetics asks how anything, a thermostat, a cell or a government, keeps itself on course while the world pushes back. Seven short lessons, each with a model you can change and a real case behind it.

κυβερνήτης (kybernetes) = steersman. Latin turned it into gubernator, and from there we get “governor” and “govern”.

01

Balancing feedback holds a course

A balancing (negative) loop pushes against change. The room is too cold, so the heater turns on. The room is warm enough, so it turns off. The size of the push depends on the gap between goal and reality.

Two knobs decide whether it behaves well. Gain is how hard the controller reacts to the gap. Delay is how old the controller’s information is. High gain plus delay is the classic recipe for hunting, the swing back and forth you know from a shower that is always too hot or too cold.

Try the presets, then open the window at minute 60 and watch what the controller can and cannot do.

Real example

James Watt’s flyball governor (1788) used this exact loop to hold an engine at constant speed. In 1868 James Clerk Maxwell wrote the first mathematical analysis of why some governors hunt and others settle. Norbert Wiener took the field’s name from the Greek word that “governor” itself derives from.

02

Reinforcing feedback amplifies

A reinforcing (positive) loop feeds a change back into itself, so more leads to more. “Positive” means it adds, not that it is good. Compound interest, a viral post and a bank run are all the same shape.

Nothing grows forever, so a reinforcing loop in the real world almost always meets a balancing loop that finally caps it. Switch the limit on and off and compare the curves. Most of what looks like “exponential growth” is the early part of an S-curve.

Real example

Arctic ice reflects sunlight. Warming melts ice, dark water absorbs more heat, and that melts more ice. Climate scientists call it the ice-albedo feedback, and it is one reason polar warming outpaces the global average.

03

Only variety can absorb variety

W. Ross Ashby’s law of requisite variety says a regulator must be able to do at least as many different things as the disturbances it faces can do to it. A help desk with 5 canned replies cannot fully handle 14 kinds of problem, however hard it works.

You have two honest options. Amplify the regulator: add responses, train people, give them more tools. Or attenuate the disturbances: group similar cases, standardize, triage. Tick the triage box and see how much the same team can then absorb.

Real example

The immune system keeps billions of distinct antibody variants in reserve because pathogens vary enormously. A chess grandmaster matches the variety of a position by pattern recognition built over years. Both are requisite variety at work.

04

A viable organization is loops inside loops

Stafford Beer asked what any organization needs in order to survive, whether a firm, a hospital or a country. His Viable System Model says five functions are enough, and each operating unit must itself contain all five. That recursion is the point.

Click each box. The model is easiest to learn by asking of your own team: who does this job here?

Real example

In 1971 Chile’s Allende government invited Beer to help run the nationalized economy. The project, Cybersyn, linked factories to Santiago by telex, filtered the numbers statistically, and gave managers a control room. It was unfinished when the 1973 coup ended it, and historians still argue about how much it did.

05

First, second and third order

“Order” says where the observer stands. Each order takes the one before it and asks who is doing the observing. The first two are standard. The third is a live argument.

First order: the cybernetics of observed systems

Von Foerster’s shortest description, from his 1991 Paris address “Ethics and Second-Order Cybernetics”:

“First-order cybernetics is the cybernetics of observed systems, while second-order cybernetics is the cybernetics of observing systems.”Heinz von Foerster, “Ethics and Second-Order Cybernetics” (1991; Cybernetics and Human Knowing 1(1), 1992)

He lists first-order’s conceptual machinery as “information,” “circularity,” “recursion,” “control,” “homeostasis,” “dynamic stability,” “fixed points,” “attractors,” “equifinality,” “purpose” and “goal.” It is the toolkit of lessons 1 to 4 on this page. Bernard Scott adds that first-order study can use the research paradigms of the natural sciences and test hypotheses by trying to falsify them. The observer stays outside, which suits engineering, and which von Foerster calls the principle of objectivity: “The properties of the observer shall not enter the description of his observations.”

Second order: the cybernetics of observing systems

Von Foerster argues the observer cannot be removed: “If the properties of the observer (namely to observe and describe) are eliminated, there is nothing left; no observation, no description.” Cyberneticians who treated observing and communicating as a circle were, in his phrase, “entering into a forbidden land”, because self-reference (A implies A) was feared as paradox.

The label has a short history. Margaret Mead presented “Cybernetics of Cybernetics” to the American Society for Cybernetics in 1967 (published 1968), and von Foerster supplied the title. His 1974 collection, Cybernetics of Cybernetics (BCL Report 73.38, University of Illinois), set out the distinction. Bernard Scott’s 2004 history in Kybernetes names the main strands: Pask’s Conversation Theory, Maturana’s autopoiesis, Glanville’s “Objects” theory, Luhmann’s social systems theory, sociocybernetics, and cybersemiotics.

Von Foerster ties this to ethics. His imperative, quoted in the 1991 text from his earlier paper “On Constructing a Reality”: “The ethical imperative: Act always so as to increase the number of choices.” In the same address he writes: “we become a metaphysician any time we decide upon in principle undecidable questions.” His point is that the questions that matter most (am I apart from the universe, or part of it?) cannot be settled by proof, so you decide and you own the decision. Later in the same text he restates the rule more softly: “they should always try to act so as to increase the number of choices.”

Third order: contested

No single definition exists. The best-documented one comes from Vladimir Lepskiy and colleagues at the Institute of Philosophy of the Russian Academy of Sciences, who have met every two years for about 20 years on “Reflexive Processes and Control”. Stuart Umpleby, with Lepskiy and Tatiana Medvedeva, summarized it in a 2018 paper. Their thesis is a move “from ‘observed systems’ to ‘observing systems’ to ‘self-developing reflexive-active environments’”, and from a subject–object paradigm to subject–subject and then subject–metasubject. Umpleby’s gloss: whereas second order stressed self-reference and a scientist’s reflection on their own understanding, third order is “the study of how a system composed of reflexive systems can itself be reflexive”, as with a society and its government.

Other authors use the term differently: systems designed to behave ethically; a general hierarchy of higher orders (Yolles’s “metacybernetics”, 2021); or systems that describe their own description. Umpleby notes the Russian reading of second-order cybernetics also differs from the Western one. Treat the table’s third column as one school’s proposal, not settled doctrine.

    Where to go from here

    • Norbert Wiener, Cybernetics (1948) and the plainer The Human Use of Human Beings (1950). The founding text and its readable sequel.
    • W. Ross Ashby, An Introduction to Cybernetics (1956). Clear, careful, and covers requisite variety. Many readers start here.
    • Stafford Beer, Brain of the Firm (1972). The Viable System Model written for managers.
    • Donella Meadows, Thinking in Systems (2008). The friendliest modern route into loops, stocks and delays.
    • Maturana and Varela, Autopoiesis and Cognition (1980). For the second-order side. Dense but rewarding.
    • Eden Medina, Cybernetic Revolutionaries (2011). A well-researched history of Cybersyn.
    The models on this page are teaching simplifications. They are meant to show the shape of each idea, not to predict any real room, economy or market.
