01

Balancing feedback holds a course

A balancing (negative) loop pushes against change. The room is too cold, so the heater turns on. The room is warm enough, so it turns off. The size of the push depends on the gap between goal and reality.

Two knobs decide whether it behaves well. Gain is how hard the controller reacts to the gap. Delay is how old the controller’s information is. High gain plus delay is the classic recipe for hunting, the swing back and forth you know from a shower that is always too hot or too cold.

Try the presets, then open the window at minute 60 and watch what the controller can and cannot do.

Real example

James Watt’s flyball governor (1788) used this exact loop to hold an engine at constant speed. In 1868 James Clerk Maxwell wrote the first mathematical analysis of why some governors hunt and others settle. Norbert Wiener took the field’s name from the Greek word that “governor” itself derives from.

02

Reinforcing feedback amplifies

A reinforcing (positive) loop feeds a change back into itself, so more leads to more. “Positive” means it adds, not that it is good. Compound interest, a viral post and a bank run are all the same shape.

Nothing grows forever, so a reinforcing loop in the real world almost always meets a balancing loop that finally caps it. Switch the limit on and off and compare the curves. Most of what looks like “exponential growth” is the early part of an S-curve.

Real example

Arctic ice reflects sunlight. Warming melts ice, dark water absorbs more heat, and that melts more ice. Climate scientists call it the ice-albedo feedback, and it is one reason polar warming outpaces the global average.

03

Only variety can absorb variety

W. Ross Ashby’s law of requisite variety says a regulator must be able to do at least as many different things as the disturbances it faces can do to it. A help desk with 5 canned replies cannot fully handle 14 kinds of problem, however hard it works.

You have two honest options. Amplify the regulator: add responses, train people, give them more tools. Or attenuate the disturbances: group similar cases, standardize, triage. Tick the triage box and see how much the same team can then absorb.

Real example

The immune system keeps billions of distinct antibody variants in reserve because pathogens vary enormously. A chess grandmaster matches the variety of a position by pattern recognition built over years. Both are requisite variety at work.

04

Ultrastability: a system that rewires itself

A thermostat holds a goal but cannot change its own rules. Ashby asked how a system could keep its essential variables (the ones it must hold within limits to survive) inside bounds even when the world changes the rules. His answer was ultrastability: a second loop that watches the essential variables and, when they leave their limits, changes the parameters of the first loop at random, until stability returns.

Ashby built the idea as a machine. His Homeostat, completed on 16 March 1948 at Barnwood House Hospital, used four linked units and found stable settings by itself. Wiener called it “one of the great philosophical contributions of the present day”, according to a historical account of the 1952 Macy conference.

The model on the right is a one-variable cartoon of the principle, not a replica of the Homeostat. The variable must stay within ±1. When it escapes, the system re-rolls its own setting k. Flip the wiring halfway and watch it find a new working setting without being told how.

Real example

A person who moves to a new country finds old habits stop working. Nothing tells them the right new habits. They vary behavior until what they need to keep steady (sleep, money, belonging) stays in range again. Bateson’s Learning II (lesson 7) is the same move at the level of habits.
